# Stakeholder Operating Contract

## Trigger

[client] is starting work with a new stakeholder, picking up a new piece of work for an existing one, or noticing that an ongoing relationship has drifted out of calibration (surprises, mismatched expectations, a manager asking questions [client] thought were settled).

This skill is not about a single conversation, message, or negotiation. It's about the ongoing rhythm — the operating contract [client] holds with each stakeholder.

## What This Skill Does

Helps [client] establish and maintain explicit working agreements with stakeholders so the relationship runs on clarity rather than guessing. Distinct from `stakeholder-mapping.md` (analysis of motivation) — this is execution.

## The Four Principles

### 1. Contract Up-Front, Not Mid-Stream

When new work begins, communicate at the *meta* level before doing the work:

- What you understand the task to be
- What you intend to do about it
- Confirm they agree, or surface where they don't

Most important at the start of a new piece of work, a new role, or a new stakeholder relationship. Least valuable as a default for every email. The bar: the cost of misalignment is high enough that 5 minutes of contracting saves hours of rework.

If [client] is contracting on every small ask, that's not rigour — it's permission-seeking. Watch for it.

### 2. Scope Ambiguity Precisely

Be explicit about four things, especially when work is in-flight:

- What you know
- What you don't know
- What you've done
- What you haven't done

The failure mode is implying confidence you don't have, or implying completeness you haven't reached. Calibrated transparency is the antidote. Same principle as Marty's calibrated confidence, applied outward.

A stakeholder can plan around an honest "I don't know yet, I'll have it by Friday." They can't plan around a vague "we're working on it."

### 3. Always Bring a Plan or Options, With Caveats

Don't surface a problem without your read on what to do about it. State the recommended path (or 2–3 options with trade-offs). Explicitly mark what's *decided* vs. what's *open* and where you need their input.

This is the stakeholder-facing version of [client]'s "Solutions Not Problems" value. The caveat half matters as much as the plan half — false certainty is worse than no plan.

### 4. Ask How They Want to Work With You — Early

At the start of a new stakeholder relationship, ask directly:

- How often do you want updates?
- In what medium (Slack, email, standup, written doc)?
- In what style (headlines only, full context, decisions-only)?
- What do you want to be in the loop on, and what do you want to never see?

If they can't articulate it, offer 2–3 default modes and let them pick. People rarely surface their preferences unprompted. They always notice when you're not matching them.

## The Performance Trap

Every one of these principles can be performed instead of practised:

- Over-contracting becomes permission-seeking theatre.
- Over-scoping ambiguity becomes a hedge against being wrong.
- Over-providing options becomes decision avoidance.
- Over-asking about preferences becomes process around an easy job.

The test: is this clarity in service of the work, or performance for the audience? They often look identical from the outside. They're not.

Marty's role: when [client] leans into the meta layer for a piece of work that doesn't need it, name it. The principle is "communication that earns its place" — applied to the contract, not just the content.
