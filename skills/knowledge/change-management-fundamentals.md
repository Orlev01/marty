## Core Knowledge

### Resistance Is Information

When people resist change, they're telling you something. The resistance is data, not an obstacle. Before trying to overcome resistance, understand it:
- Is it fear of the unknown? (Provide clarity and small wins)
- Is it loss of control? (Give them ownership of how, not whether)
- Is it legitimate concern? (Address the concern — they might be right)
- Is it political? (Someone's turf is threatened — map the stakeholders)
- Is it capacity? (They're already overloaded — you're asking for more)

### The Burning Platform Is Not Enough

"We must change or we'll fail" creates urgency but not direction. People need to see what the new world looks like, believe they can get there, and have a first step they can take today. Urgency without clarity produces anxiety, not action.

### Coalition of the Willing First

Don't try to convert the sceptics first. Find the people who are already enthusiastic, give them support and visibility, and let their results create social proof. The sceptics come around when they see peers succeeding, not when a program lead tells them to.

### Influence Without Authority

When you don't have positional power:
- **Currency of credibility.** You earn influence by delivering results. Early wins matter more than grand plans.
- **Borrow authority.** Your exec sponsors exist for this. When you need a door opened, use them — but sparingly. Every time you pull the exec lever, you spend some of it.
- **Make it easy.** Reduce friction to zero. If adopting the tool requires 12 steps, you've lost. If it's already installed and configured when they open their laptop, you've won.
- **Make it visible.** People adopt what they see others adopting. Public dashboards, Slack showcases, all-hands demos.
- **Make refusal expensive.** Not through punishment — through social pressure and removing alternatives. When the old tool is deprecated and everyone around you is using the new one, holding out feels like swimming upstream.

### The Middle Management Problem

Department heads and team leads are the critical layer. They can amplify or kill any change initiative. If they're not bought in, their teams won't be bought in. Investing in middle management alignment is higher-leverage than investing in individual contributor training.

### Kotter's Model (Simplified, Not Worshipped)

Relevant because the phases map to common mistakes:
1. Create urgency (done — exec sponsorship, metric, timeline)
2. Build a coalition (champions, department heads)
3. Form a vision (the "golden path" — what daily AI usage looks like)
4. Communicate the vision (signal over noise — real use cases, not slide decks)
5. Remove obstacles (tooling friction, privacy blockers, SSO issues)
6. Create short-term wins (lighthouses, hero use cases)
7. Build on the wins (expand from early adopters to mainstream)
8. Anchor in culture (hiring criteria, onboarding, performance reviews)

Don't cite Kotter by name unless [client] asks. Use the phases as a diagnostic — "which phase are you stuck in?" — not as a framework to follow linearly.

## Out of Scope

- Detailed training curriculum design (that's the enablement lead's job)
- Specific tool configuration or technical implementation
- HR or legal compliance requirements for organisational change
- Therapy or emotional processing for change fatigue
