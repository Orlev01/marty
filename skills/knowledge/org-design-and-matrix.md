## Core Knowledge

### Why Matrices Exist

Matrix organisations exist because companies need both functional depth (engineering, product, design) and business-unit focus (brands, markets, products). Neither a pure functional structure nor a pure business-unit structure works alone. The matrix is a compromise, and all compromises have costs.

The cost of a matrix: ambiguous authority, competing priorities, slower decision-making, and the constant question of "who decides." The benefit: the ability to deploy functional expertise across business units without duplicating it.

### Influence Without Authority

This is [client]'s operating reality. Principles:

**Credibility is currency.** In a matrix, you earn the right to influence through delivered results, not through title. Early wins — visible, tangible, attributable to you — build the credibility that funds later asks.

**Understand the incentives.** Department heads are measured on their department's outcomes, not on your initiative's outcomes. To get their support, frame your initiative in terms of what it does for *their* metrics, not yours. "AI adoption helps the support team reduce ticket response time" is more compelling to the support lead than "AI adoption hits 95% DAU."

**Build coalitions before you need them.** Don't wait until you have a blocker to build relationships with department heads. Regular check-ins, sharing credit, and being useful to them on their priorities creates goodwill you can draw on later.

**Use sponsors surgically.** Exec sponsors (CTO, COO) are a resource, not a weapon. Use them to open doors and remove structural blockers, not to override department decisions. Every time you escalate to your sponsor, you spend some of the political capital your sponsor has with their peers.

**Make it easy to say yes.** When you need something from a department, come with the solution, not the problem. "I need a champion from your team — here's what the commitment looks like, here's what they get out of it, I've already identified two candidates if you want to discuss" is better than "I need a champion from your team."

### The Champion Network as Matrix Infrastructure

In [client]'s context, the champion network is the matrix infrastructure. Champions are the connection points between the central program ([client]) and the departments. Their effectiveness depends on:
- Clear mandate from their department head (not just [client])
- Protected time (e.g. a 10% allocation agreed in the program strategy)
- Visible support from both [client] and their department head
- A feedback loop that makes their work feel impactful, not bureaucratic

Champion attrition is the biggest structural risk. Plan for rotation and succession from day one.

### Decision Rights in a Matrix

The most common source of matrix dysfunction is unclear decision rights. For every recurring decision, someone needs to be able to answer:
- Who decides?
- Who must be consulted before the decision?
- Who must be informed after the decision?
- Who can veto?

If these aren't clear, every decision becomes a negotiation. [client]'s job is to establish these for the AI rollout early and get them blessed by sponsors.

### The Acquisition Overlay

When a matrix organisation is acquired (a larger company acquiring yours), a new layer of matrix complexity arrives: integration teams, dual reporting, cultural differences, and the question of which structures survive. Initiatives launched during an acquisition are politically sensitive — they can be seen as empire-building, as premature, or as smart positioning. [client] should be aware of the optics even if they're focused on impact.
